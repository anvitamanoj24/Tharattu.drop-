Project Development Journey

Problems We Faced

Building our project wasn't a straight line from idea to working prototype. As CS students stepping into hardware, every working component came with a little debugging adventure.

Hour 01

Entering the Hardware World

Our team comes from a CS background, so working with sensors, motors, GPIO pins, external power supplies and physical wiring was completely new territory.

Unlike software debugging, where an error usually points somewhere in the code, hardware debugging meant checking wires, voltages, grounds and connections one by one.

Challenge: Understanding how software and hardware actually communicate.

Hour 03

The Silent Microphone

The INMP441 microphone was connected, but the ESP32 wasn't receiving meaningful audio data. The Serial Monitor remained almost flat even when we made loud noises around the microphone.

We went through the usual suspects:

  • Wiring and pin connections
  • I2S configuration
  • Sample rate
  • Input data configuration
  • Serial output

After several rounds of testing and debugging, the microphone finally started responding correctly.

Lesson: In hardware, “the code is running” doesn't necessarily mean “the system is working.”
Hour 06

The Servo That Refused to Move

Next came the servo motor. The code was being executed, the servo was connected, but the motor wasn't behaving reliably.

After tracing the power and signal connections, we found the actual problem: the external power supply and ESP32 didn't share a common ground.

Once the servo power GND was connected to the ESP32 GND, the servo immediately started responding as expected.

Key Fix: Common ground between the external power supply and ESP32.
Lesson: Sometimes the bug isn't in the code. It's literally in the ground.
Hour 10

Lights, Sound & Integration

Once the individual components started working, the next challenge was making them work together.

We integrated:

  • RGB LED effects
  • Cry-detection trigger
  • Servo movement
  • Music/lullaby playback

The difficulty wasn't getting each component to work individually. It was coordinating them without one process interfering with another.

We had to think about timing, triggering and how multiple hardware actions should respond to a single cry-detection event.

Challenge: Turning independent components into one coordinated system.

Hour 14

Training the Brain

Hardware was only half the battle. We also needed the system to distinguish between a baby cry and non-cry sounds.

Training the ML model introduced a completely different set of problems:

  • Collecting enough audio samples
  • Maintaining good-quality recordings
  • Correctly labeling cry and non-cry sounds
  • Reducing false detections
  • Keeping the model lightweight enough for ESP32 deployment

Getting a model that simply works is one thing. Getting one that works reliably on a small embedded device is another.

Lesson: Good AI starts with good data, not just a fancy model.
Hour 18

From Individual Fixes to One System

After debugging the microphone, servo, lighting, audio and ML pipeline individually, we began connecting everything into the final workflow.

Cry detected → ESP32 processes the signal → LCD responds → servo activates → RGB lights react → music plays.

This was the point where our project stopped looking like a collection of components and started behaving like an actual system.

What We Learned

The biggest challenge wasn't any single component. It was learning to move from software thinking to systems thinking.

We had to debug code, circuits, power, timing, machine learning and hardware integration together.

And somewhere along the way, a group of CS students became people who could look at a pile of wires and say: “Okay. Let's find out why this isn't working.”
